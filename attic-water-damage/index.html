Attic Water Damage: What West Allis Homeowners Should Know

Attic water damage comes from two directions. From above, rain enters through failed flashing, worn shingles, or the seals at chimneys and vents. The house itself sends moisture upward too, and in a poorly ventilated attic it condenses on cold sheathing. In both cases, almost nobody climbs up to check. For West Allis homeowners, our crew examines the attic, identifies where the water comes from, and dries the space.

Most attic jobs center on wet insulation. Heating and cooling bills go up because cellulose and fiberglass alike give up R-value when wet. Even worse, it keeps water pressed against the framing and the ceiling drywall for weeks. We remove what is saturated, dry the wood with air movers and dehumidifiers, and treat any mold on the rafters or sheathing.

Warning Signs

  • Fuzzy white or black growth on the sheathing, usually thickest by the eaves
  • Dark stains or streaks on the rafters and the underside of the roof deck
  • Flattened, matted or discolored insulation that stands out from what surrounds it
  • Rusty nail tips poking through the sheathing, sometimes with water beads on them
  • Water dripping from, or staining around, a ceiling light or bathroom fan
  • Daylight showing around a vent pipe, chimney, or along a roof valley
Why Speed Matters

What Happens if You Wait

First storm

Insulation Soaks It Up

The insulation catches and absorbs whatever rain gets by the roofing. With no sign inside the house, nobody notices the leak and the roof stays unrepaired.

Hours 24 to 48

Mold Can Start

Damp wood and paper facing in a warm, still attic are ideal for mold. Growth can begin on the sheathing and on the back of the ceiling drywall.

Weeks later

Stains Come Through

Once storm after storm has saturated the insulation, the ceiling starts getting wet. Brown rings appear in the room below, and the drywall may start to sag.

After months

Rot Sets In

The sheathing comes apart in layers and no longer grips the roofing nails. Mold spreads across large sections of the roof deck, and the rafters remain damp enough to decay.

What We Do

How We Handle Attic Water Damage

1

Inspect and Find the Source

To learn whether you have a leak, condensation or the two together, we go over the roof deck, the vents, the exhaust ducts and each flashing point. Thermal imaging and moisture meters show how far it reached.

2

Take Out Wet Insulation

Insulation that is soaked or has mold gets bagged and carried out, giving the wood and the drywall below a chance to dry. Rooms under the attic are protected, and the route in and out stays contained.

3

Dry the Wood

Air movers and dehumidifiers bring the rafters, joists and sheathing back to normal moisture levels. An antimicrobial goes on after any visible mold has been cleaned from the wood.

4

Correct the Source

We point out the roof or ventilation problem that needs correcting and can share our findings with your roofer. New insulation is installed to bring back the R-value after the attic is staying dry.

What You Cannot See

What Hides Behind It

Water Near Electrical

Cable runs, junction boxes and recessed light housings are all found in attics. When they get wet there is a risk of shock and fire, so keep out until the power has been checked.

Insulation That Stops Working

Compressed, damp insulation does very little insulating, and it may not recover after it dries. The rooms beneath that section get colder or hotter, and you pay more for energy.

Leaks From Attic Equipment

Many attics hold an air handler, a drain pan and condensate lines. When the pan overflows or the drain clogs, insulation gets wet for weeks without any rain as a warning.

Mold Under the Roof Deck

With poor ventilation, mold commonly grows on the bottom of the roof deck. A wide area may be covered by the time somebody climbs up to check.
